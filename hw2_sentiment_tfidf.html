Step 3: Compute raw sentiment totals

Keeping only the two texts we are comparing (Circle of Commerce & Free Trade), then collapsing to one row per document with positive, negative, and net sentiment counts.

raw_sentiment <- tokens_sentiment %>%
  filter(doc_title %in% c("Circle of Commerce", "Free Trade")) %>%

Step 7: Compute TF-IDF-weighted sentiment totals

Summing the TF-IDF weights (not counting words) of positive and negative words for each document, then calculating net TF-IDF sentiment.

tfidf_sentiment_summary <- tfidf_sentiment %>%
  group_by(doc_title, sentiment) %>%
  summarise(total_tfidf = sum(tfidf), .groups = "drop") %>%
  pivot_wider(
    names_from  = sentiment,
    values_from = total_tfidf,
    values_fill = 0
  ) %>%
  rename(
    tfidf_positive = positive,
    tfidf_negative = negative
  ) %>%
  mutate(tfidf_net = tfidf_positive - tfidf_negative)

  1. What changed between the two methods?

The direction of the sentiment flipped. With raw counts, both texts came out slightly positive: Circle of Commerce at +67 (489 positive, 422 negative) and Free Trade at +63 (344 positive, 281 negative). Going by that alone, I would have said the two texts have almost the same tone. Once I weighted the words by TF-IDF, both texts turned negative. Circle of Commerce came to −23.31 and Free Trade at −11.50. The gap between them also got bigger. Under TF-IDF, Circle of Commerce carries about twice as much net negative weight as Free Trade, while under raw counts they were nearly equal.

  1. Why did TF-IDF alter the results?

TF-IDF gives zero weight to any word that shows up in all three texts, and rewards words that only show up in one or two. That turned out to matter a lot here, because many of the most common positive words are shared across all three documents. Words like “free” (29 times in Circle of Commerce), “remedy” (40 times in Free Trade) and “treasure” counted heavily in the raw method, but got a weight of zero under TF-IDF. Once those were gone, what was left were more rare words, and more of them were negative (164 negative vs 108 positive distinct words in Circle of Commerce, and 90 vs 74 in Free Trade). This does not mean the texts are negative. It means that the sentiment words each text uses on its own are mostly negative, while the sentiment words all three texts share are mostly positive. Also, with only three documents, IDF can only take three values. So a word used just a few times in one text can still get a high weight.

  1. Which specific words drove the changes?

I see two groups. The first is the frequent positive words that all three texts share and that TF-IDF wiped out: “free”, “treasure”, “benefit”, “remedy” and “liberty”. Several of these probably belong to the economic vocabulary of the debate rather than expressing any real feeling, which makes me question how much they should have counted in the raw score to begin with. The second group is the rare words that gained weight. In Circle of Commerce, the top negative words were “impertinent”, “imperfect” and “meddle”, and the top positive ones were “passion”, “divine” and “affection”. In Free Trade, the top negative words were “imposition” and “disorderly”, and the top positive ones were “peace” and “happy”. A lot of these have nothing to do with trade. Some also look misread by the Bing lexicon. “Imposition”, for example, could mean a duty on goods in the 1620s, but Bing treats it as negative word.

  1. When would each method be more appropriate? What limitation does document length create for the raw-count measure?

Raw counts are better when I want to know how much positive and negative language a text uses in total, including the common words it shares with the other texts. TF-IDF makes more sense when I want to know what is distinctive about one text’s sentiment compared to the others, for example how Misselden’s tone differs from his rivals. I would not use TF-IDF to say how positive or negative one particular document is, and its results depend heavily on which documents are in the corpus. The problem with raw counts is length. A longer text will simply have more positive and more negative words. After cleaning, Circle of Commerce has 12,972 words and Free Trade has 8,024. Their raw net scores look almost the same (+67 vs +63), but per 1,000 words, Free Trade is actually more positive (about 7.9 vs 5.2). Dividing by document length would make the raw comparison fairer.
